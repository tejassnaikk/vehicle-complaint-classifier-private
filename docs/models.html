Models

Three neural networks, from simple to large, trained on the same text, the same labels and the same splits.

What we predict, and why these three models

The task is text classification. The input is one complaint text, and the output is one of the 10 part groups from the Data & Prep page. The models see only the text. They do not get the make, model, year or date. We chose three models of increasing size so we could ask how much each step up buys: the MLP ignores word order, the BiLSTM reads in order, and DistilBERT starts from a language model trained on a large amount of general English.

All three share the same label encoding, splits and training rules. Each trains on the training set and stops early on validation macro-F1, the average of the per-class F1 scores. The best checkpoint on validation is kept, and the test set is scored once at the end, so nothing was tuned on test. The seed is 42 everywhere. Macro-F1 gives every class the same weight, which matters here because three classes are smaller than the others.

Same rules for every model

Same text, same labels, same splits. Early stopping on validation macro-F1, and the test set scored once at the end. Nothing was tuned on test.

How it works. TF-IDF turns each complaint into a long list of numbers, one per word or two-word phrase. A word scores high when it is frequent in this complaint and rare across all complaints, so “steering” counts for more than “the”. The vocabulary is fit on the training texts only. A small network with two hidden layers learns which weighted words point to which class. It cannot see word order.

Why we chose it. It is the baseline. It trains in about 22 seconds, so it shows how far plain word counts go before anything more complex is needed.

How it works. Each word becomes a vector of 128 numbers that the model learns from scratch. Two LSTM layers then read the complaint from first word to last and from last to first, keeping a running memory of what they have seen. We take the largest and the average value of that memory over all word positions and feed both to a final layer. Padding is masked, so it cannot affect the pooling.

Why we chose it. It is the classic way to read text in order. It can use phrases like “will not start” where the order carries meaning, which TF-IDF cannot. It trains from scratch, so it has no outside knowledge of English.

How it works. DistilBERT is a smaller version of BERT, a language model trained on a large collection of English text before we touched it. It has 6 Transformer layers, and in each one every word looks at every other word to decide what it means in context. We add a small classifier on top and train the whole network on our complaints for up to 3 epochs with a low learning rate. That step is called fine-tuning. It has 66,961,162 parameters.

Why we chose it. It brings general knowledge of English, which helps with owners who describe a symptom without naming the part. It is also small enough to train on a laptop in about 49 minutes. Texts longer than 256 tokens are cut.
