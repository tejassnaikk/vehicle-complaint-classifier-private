Lab notebook

The decisions and findings that shaped this project, in the order we made them, each with the number that mattered.

Most of the work was not training models. It was deciding what counts as a complaint, what counts as an answer, and what to remove so that the score means something. This is the short version of our project log, written for a general reader.

  1. Step 1Scope

    We started with everything since 2020

    NHTSA publishes its owner complaints as plain text files. We took the two files that cover January 2020 to the start of November 2026. They hold 629,231 rows, and the rows describe 432,893 separate complaints because a complaint that names several parts takes one row per part.

    432,893complaints in the raw files
  2. Step 2Labels

    We set aside complaints that name more than one part

    A model that picks one answer cannot be scored fairly on a complaint with two correct answers. We kept only complaints filed under a single system, which left 304,879. That rule removed 128,014 complaints, so our results say nothing about them.

    128,014complaints set aside (29.6% of the file)
  3. Step 3Labels

    We merged two names for the same system, and kept two that look alike

    NHTSA renamed some groups during the years we use. “Engine and engine cooling” (7,939 complaints) became part of our engine group, and the older “fuel system” (5,377) became part of the fuel and propulsion group. “Visibility” (3,100) stayed separate from “visibility and wiper” (8,598), because both names were in use at the same time and described different problems. We kept 10 groups.

    10groups of vehicle parts to tell apart
  4. Step 4Leak

    The answer was hiding in the text

    Before training anything we listed the words that best separated each group. A word pair such as ( air showed up in 3,601 complaints, and 3,554 of them were air bags complaints. NHTSA call-centre staff had typed the part name into the text, in a phrase about a recall campaign. A model could read the label instead of the symptom. We removed the phrase and every component name in brackets.

    27,424texts had the part name removed
  5. Step 5Data

    We dropped recall calls with no failure

    Some complaints are calls about whether a recall part was in stock, and the text says the owner had not experienced a failure. They hold no symptom to read. We dropped 10,295 of them, which is 4.5% of the complaints in our ten groups. We also dropped 1,690 exact duplicates and 18,575 texts under 20 words.

    10,295recall-call complaints dropped
  6. Step 6Split

    We tested on complaints from after the training period

    A random split would let the model train on complaints from the same months it is tested on. We made the test set the last six months, May to October 2026, and trained on everything before November 2025. As a check we also ran a random split. It scored 1.1 to 1.3 points higher, so mixing the months would have flattered every model a little.

    1.1 to 1.3points higher on a random split
  7. Step 7Privacy

    We hid phone numbers and addresses typed into the text

    Owners sometimes type a phone number, an address, an e-mail address or a vehicle identification number into the complaint. We replaced these with markers such as [PHONE]. The rule changed 7,497 texts. A scan of the published files then found 10 texts with phone numbers written in two formats we had missed. We masked them after training. The models saw those digits, and none of those complaints is in a test set.

    7,497texts had contact details masked
  8. Step 8Models

    We trained three models and kept the best epoch

    The MLP took 22 seconds to train, the BiLSTM 33 minutes and DistilBERT 49 minutes on a laptop. Each model stopped on the epoch with the best score on a separate validation set, and we scored the test set once. The MLP peaked after epoch 1, which is a sign it memorizes the training texts quickly.

    49 minto train DistilBERT, the largest model
  9. Step 9Run

    A run stalled, and we ran everything again

    The first DistilBERT run on the random split stalled overnight when the laptop went to sleep. We stopped it, restarted the laptop and ran it again with the same settings and seed. Later we re-ran the whole pipeline after adding the contact-detail masking. A second launch was also stopped by hand and resumed from the DistilBERT step. After the re-run, no test score moved by more than 0.0032, and the ranking of the models did not change.

    0.0032largest change in any test score after the re-run
  10. Step 10Tests

    We checked that the best model is better, not lucky

    With 7,320 test complaints, a gap of a point or two could be chance. We compared the models on the same complaints with McNemar's test and resampled the test set 1,000 times. DistilBERT beat both other models, with p-values of 7e-11 and 3e-17. The MLP and the BiLSTM could not be told apart, with a p-value of 0.107.

    0.107p-value for MLP against BiLSTM: no real difference
